\title[In RAG We Trust?]{In RAG We Trust? A Controlled Measurement of How Open
Language Models Handle Poisoned Retrieval}

\author*[1]{\fnm{Angela} \sur{Aldaoud}}\email{angela.aldaoud@studenti.unimi.it}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{M.Sc.\ in Computer Science (LM-18)},
\orgname{Universit\`a degli Studi di Milano}, \orgaddress{\city{Milan},
\country{Italy}}}

\abstract{Retrieval-augmented generation trusts the passages it retrieves. We
measure how three open instruction-tuned models of 7--8 billion parameters
(Qwen2.5, Llama-3.1, Mistral) answer 500 multi-hop HotpotQA questions when
none to all of the answer-bearing passages are replaced by poisoned twins, and
whether prompts asking the model to check the passages for consistency or
reliability help, with scoring rules and confirmatory tests fixed in advance.
Attack success rises from almost zero to
\AsrRangeRhoOneMin--\AsrRangeRhoOneMax\,\% as poisoning grows, overtakes
accuracy once the false passages are the majority, and overrides answers the
models give correctly without retrieval. The meta-prompts change attack
success by only \FTwoAsrLlamaRel{} to \FTwoAsrMistralRel{} points, mostly
through abstention, and cost one model accuracy on clean evidence. Responses
that state a conflict are less often poisoned, an association rather than a
defence, and the poison's framing barely matters.}

\keywords{retrieval-augmented generation, knowledge conflicts, data poisoning,
robustness, evaluation of language models}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) lets a language model answer with
passages that a retriever selects from documents the model was not trained on
\citep{lewis2020rag}. A RAG system is therefore only as reliable as what it
retrieves, and it implicitly trusts the retrieved text. That trust is easy to
break: \citet{zou2024poisonedrag} showed that five crafted texts per target
question, injected into a knowledge base of millions of documents, make a RAG
system return the attacker's answer in about 90\% of cases, and a recent
survey observes that most work on RAG has targeted accuracy and efficiency
rather than factuality and robustness \citep{zhou2024trustworthiness}.

Two empirical studies are closest to ours. \citet{jin2024tugofwar} found that
models tend to follow the answer supported by the majority of retrieved
passages and show confirmation bias, and \citet{wu2024clasheval} that models
adopted perturbed answers over a correct prior more than 60\% of the time,
less often the less realistic the error.

These studies establish that language models can be misled by retrieved text.
We ask a narrower, practical question: \emph{how reliably do open,
instruction-tuned language models answer when part of the retrieved evidence
is deliberately false, and can simple prompt-level instructions make them
more reliable?} A user of an open model cannot retrain it but can change what
it is told, so whether an instruction to check the passages protects, and at
what cost, is worth measuring. We also record whether the model states that
the passages conflict, since noticing a conflict and resisting it differ.

Our contribution is a measurement, not a new method. We use 500 multi-hop
questions from HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}, with contexts in which the
share of false passages ranges from none to all, four kinds of poison that
share one false answer and differ by a framing sentence, and three prompts
that differ by one sentence; three open 7--8B models answer every condition,
and a lexical retriever over a poisoned collection shows how often such
contexts arise. Scoring rules and confirmatory tests were fixed before the
main run and validated by hand on a pilot set. We find that all three models
follow the majority of the passages, that the meta-prompts lower attack
success mainly by making the models abstain, and that stating a conflict goes
with resisting it but does not guarantee it.

\section{Research Question and Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Research questions and hypotheses}\label{sec:rqs}
We split the main question into four research questions. \textbf{RQ1}
(robustness): how do accuracy and attack success change as the share of
poisoned passages grows from none to all? \textbf{RQ2} (meta-prompts): do
prompts asking the model to check the passages for consistency, or to judge
their reliability, lower attack success, and at what cost on clean contexts?
\textbf{RQ3} (conflict awareness): how often do models state that the passages
conflict, and are such responses less often poisoned? \textbf{RQ4}
(secondary): do these answers differ between poison types and models?

The hypotheses were fixed before any experiment. \textbf{H1}: attack success
rises with the poison ratio, most steeply where the poisoned passages stop
being outnumbered \citep{jin2024tugofwar}, and is high with all passages
poisoned even for questions answered correctly without passages
\citep{wu2024clasheval}. \textbf{H2} (our expectation): meta-prompts help
mainly when clean and poisoned passages are mixed, little when all are
poisoned, and cost some accuracy and abstentions on clean contexts.
\textbf{H3} (our expectation): conflict statements peak at intermediate
ratios, and flagged responses are less often poisoned. \textbf{H4}: more
plausible or authoritative poisons are more effective \citep{wu2024clasheval};
models differ, in no predicted direction. The test of H1 concerns the rise
only; RQ3 is an association, since the experimenter does not control whether a
model states a conflict.

\subsection{Problem definition}\label{sec:formal}
Each question $q_i$ ($i=1,\dots,N$) has a gold answer $a^{*}_i$, one false
\emph{target answer} $\tilde a_i\neq a^{*}_i$, $k=4$ clean answer-bearing
passages $c_{i1},\dots,c_{i4}$ (the answer passage and three paraphrases), a
bridge passage $b_i$ that is never poisoned, and, for each poison type $\tau$,
a poisoned twin $\tilde c^{\,\tau}_{ij}$ of each clean passage that supports
$\tilde a_i$ and does not contain $a^{*}_i$. For
$\rho\in\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$, the $m=\rho k$ poisoned slots are the first
$m$ entries of a random permutation $\sigma_i$ drawn once per question:
\begin{equation}
C_{\rho,\tau}(q_i)=\varphi_i\big(p_{i1},\dots,p_{i4},\,b_i\big),\qquad
p_{ij}=\begin{cases}\tilde c^{\,\tau}_{ij} & \text{if } \sigma_i^{-1}(j)\le m,\\
c_{ij} & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\end{equation}
with a passage order $\varphi_i$ fixed per question. The poisoned sets are
nested in $\rho$ and shared by all types, prompts and models, so raising
$\rho$ replaces one clean passage by its own twin and nothing else. A
condition $\theta=(M,\pi,\tau,\rho)$ (model, prompt, type, ratio) yields for
each question one outcome in $\{\textsc{Correct},\textsc{Poisoned},
\textsc{Both},\textsc{OtherWrong},\textsc{Abstain},\textsc{NoAnswer}\}$ and a
conflict flag, set when the response says that the passages disagree or may be
unreliable. Accuracy (Acc), the attack success rate (ASR, the share of
\textsc{Poisoned}) and the abstention, missing-answer and flag rates are
shares of all $N$ responses, and all comparisons are paired.

\subsection{Data and poisoned contexts}\label{sec:data}
From the HotpotQA development set \citep{yang2018hotpotqa} (distractor
setting) we keep questions whose answer is not \emph{yes} or \emph{no},
appears in exactly one supporting paragraph (the answer passage), and whose
label is bridge (4{,}672 of 7{,}405), and draw with a fixed seed 50 pilot
questions, used only to build and validate the scoring, and 500 disjoint main
questions. Gemma-2-9B-it, a model we do not evaluate, wrote the paraphrases
and generated the target answers, both checked automatically and by hand
(details in the repository). The four poison types replace every occurrence
of $a^{*}$ by $\tilde a$; \textsc{Swap} changes nothing else, while
\textsc{Correct}, \textsc{Authority} and \textsc{Recency} append one templated
sentence claiming that other sources are wrong, that the fact is officially
confirmed, or that the passage is the latest update. Passage order and the
bridge position are random per question and recorded, since models use the
start and end of a context more than the middle \citep{liu2024lost}. A
retrieval check runs BM25 (top five, seeded tie-breaking) over all clean,
poisoned and bridge passages plus the distractor paragraphs, a poisoned-corpus
setting \citep{zou2024poisonedrag}. An audit of \AuditCandidates{} questions
with warning signs flagged \AuditFlagged{} (\AuditGoldErrors{} wrong gold
answer, \AuditComparison{} comparison questions past the bridge filter); all
stay in the primary analysis.

\subsection{Models, prompts and decoding}\label{sec:models}
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 run
in bfloat16 with vLLM on one A100 GPU. Each prompt holds the instruction, the
numbered passages, the question and an output instruction to reason briefly
and end with ``Answer: $\langle$answer$\rangle$'' or ``Answer: unknown''. The
plain prompt only asks to answer from the passages; the consistency prompt
adds ``First check whether the passages agree with each other, then
answer.''; the reliability prompt adds ``First judge how reliable each passage
is, then answer.'' Models are never told that passages may be false. Besides
48 poisoned and 3 clean conditions per model there are a closed-book and 12
retrieval conditions; greedy decoding gives the primary results, and five
samples at temperature 0.7 measure self-consistency (512-token cap).

\subsection{Scoring and statistical analysis}\label{sec:scoring}
The answer after the last ``Answer:'' is matched to the gold and target
answers by whole-word containment, and ordered rules assign the outcome
(rules in the repository). A blind pilot audit found the \textsc{Correct},
\textsc{Poisoned} and \textsc{Abstain} rules exact but half of the
\textsc{OtherWrong} answers to be aliases \citep{kamalloo2023evaluating}, so
rule-based accuracy is a lower bound; an LLM judge that failed a check fixed in
advance gives an exploratory upper bound. Gemma-2-9B-it, reading the response
alone, assigns the conflict flag ($\kappa=0.735$ against 300 hand labels,
0.57 for Qwen); as it over-flags, flags are read against each model's
$\rho=0$ baseline. A hallucination is an \textsc{OtherWrong} answer with a name
or number found in no passage (precision 9/13; 0 of 18 inventions missed).
Self-consistency is the share of five samples agreeing with the most frequent
answer.

Four confirmatory families, fixed before the main run, use Holm's correction
within each ($\alpha=0.05$): \textbf{F1} (H1), per model, the effect of $\rho$
on \textsc{Poisoned} (logistic GEE clustered by question); \textbf{F2} (H2),
exact McNemar tests of each meta-prompt against the plain prompt, per model,
on \textsc{Poisoned} at $\rho>0$ and on \textsc{Correct} and \textsc{Abstain}
at $\rho=0$; \textbf{F3} (H3), per model, the odds ratio of \textsc{Poisoned}
for flagged against unflagged responses, adjusted for $\rho$, prompt and type;
\textbf{F4} (H4), the interactions of $\rho$ and prompt with type and model.
Everything else is exploratory, with Wilson or question-clustered bootstrap
intervals. $N=500$ was kept although a pilot power check left a quarter of
comparisons below 0.80 power for five points, so a non-significant cell is
never read as no effect.

\subsection{Revisions to the planned design}\label{sec:revisions}
Five rules changed after pilot checks and before any main-run response (target
generation, output cap, BM25 tie-breaking, two added outcomes, the judge as an
upper bound); none touched the research questions, hypotheses, factors or
tests. Each is documented with the check that triggered it in the project
notes.

\section{Experimental Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}
All 192 conditions ran on one A100 GPU in 39.1 compute units, giving 576{,}000
completions (96{,}000 greedy, 480{,}000 sampled) and 188{,}047 judge labels,
with no resumed run. Each request had its own seed; with one GPU type,
residual hardware non-determinism remains. The raw outputs had the same
SHA-256 fingerprint on the cloud machine and locally, and were scored by the
evaluator frozen at tag \texttt{evaluator-v1}. Estimates with intervals are in Tables T1--T3 of the
repository.

\subsection{Robustness to poison (RQ1)}\label{sec:rq1}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig1_acc_asr_vs_rho.pdf}
\caption{Accuracy (open markers) and ASR (filled) against $\rho$ per model,
pooled over prompts and types, with 95\% Wilson intervals. Dotted: closed-book
accuracy; vertical lines: first $\rho$ with ASR above accuracy (solid: rules;
dashed: upper bound).}
\label{fig:rq1}
\end{figure}
Every model follows the passages (Fig.~\ref{fig:rq1}): from $\rho=0$ to
$\rho=1$, ASR rises from \AsrRangeRhoZeroMin--\AsrRangeRhoZeroMax\,\% to
\AsrRangeRhoOneMin--\AsrRangeRhoOneMax\,\% and accuracy falls from
\AccRangeRhoZeroMin--\AccRangeRhoZeroMax\,\% to
\AccRangeRhoOneMin--\AccRangeRhoOneMax\,\%. F1 is significant for every model
after Holm's correction, with odds ratios of \FOneOrQwen--\FOneOrMistral{}
between $\rho=0$ and $\rho=1$, large because almost no answer is poisoned at
$\rho=0$. The steepest rise (exploratory) lies between
$\rho=\StepFromMistral$ and $\StepToMistral$ for Mistral and Qwen
(+\StepMaxMistral{} and +\StepMaxQwen{} points), when the poison becomes the
majority, and between $\StepFromLlama$ and $\StepToLlama$ for Llama
(+\StepMaxLlama). H1 placed it where the poison stops being outnumbered,
$\rho=0.5$, which with four passages is a 2--2 tie: the rise holds, its
location does not, and H1 is \emph{partly supported}. Its last part holds: at
$\rho=1$ accuracy is below closed-book accuracy
(\ClosedAccMin--\ClosedAccMax\,\%), and questions answered correctly without
passages are still poisoned in \PriorGoldAsrRhoOneMin--\PriorGoldAsrRhoOneMax\,\%
of cases. Under the rules ASR first exceeds accuracy at $\rho=\CrossRulesLlama$
for every model; the judge-based upper bound adds
\BrAccGapMin--\BrAccGapMax{} points to accuracy (at most \BrAsrGapMax{} to
ASR) and moves Llama's crossover to $\rho=\CrossUpperLlama$. Missing answer
lines matter only for Llama (\NoAnswerLlama\,\%, \NoAnswerTruncLlama{} points
of them from the 512-token cap).

\subsection{Effect of meta-prompts (RQ2)}\label{sec:rq2}
Over all poisoned conditions the meta-prompts lower ASR in \FTwoAsrSig{} of
\FTwoAsrTests{} confirmatory comparisons (F2), changing it by \FTwoAsrLlamaRel{} to
\FTwoAsrMistralRel{} points; for Qwen the reliability prompt shows no
detectable effect (+\FTwoAsrQwenRel, 95\% CI \FTwoAsrQwenRelLo{} to
\FTwoAsrQwenRelHi), which rules out large effects but not small ones. On clean
contexts abstention rises in \FTwoAbstUp{} of \FTwoAbstTests{} comparisons
(+\FTwoAbstMistralCons{} to +\FTwoAbstMistralRel{} points), and accuracy falls
significantly only for Mistral (\FTwoAccMistralCons{} points with the
consistency prompt, \FTwoAccMistralRel{} with the reliability prompt). The
per-$\rho$ differences (exploratory; figure in the repository) are not
concentrated at intermediate $\rho$ for every model: for Mistral with the
reliability prompt the reduction grows with $\rho$ and is largest at $\rho=1$,
where no clean passage is left to compare against, and Llama with the
reliability prompt changes only at $\rho=1$. Of the answers poisoned under the
plain prompt, \TransStayMin--\TransStayMax\,\% stay poisoned under a
meta-prompt; for Mistral with the reliability prompt the others are mostly
abstentions (\TransMistralRelAbstain\,\%, against \TransMistralRelCorrect\,\%
correct). The prompts reduce ASR mostly by refusing, not by recovering the true
answer, and H2 is \emph{partly supported}. Single cells are noisy: the median
minimum detectable difference is \MddMedian{} points, and \MddUnderpowered{}
of \MddCells{} cells have under 80\% power for five points.

\subsection{Conflict awareness (RQ3)}\label{sec:rq3}
With $\rho$, prompt and type fixed, flagged responses are less often poisoned
for all three models (F3, significant after Holm's correction; odds ratios
\FThreeOrMistral--\FThreeOrLlama), yet flagged answers are still poisoned in
\FThreeAsrFlaggedMistral--\FThreeAsrFlaggedQwen\,\% of cases: the test shows
an association, not that noticing a conflict prevents poisoning. Flags are
read against each model's clean baseline
(\FlagBaseMistral--\FlagBaseLlama\,\%, partly inflated by the judge's
over-flagging). Above it they peak at $\rho=0.5$, where clean and poisoned
passages are tied (+\FlagDiffMistralRhoHalf{} to +\FlagDiffLlamaRhoHalf{}
points), and fall at $\rho=1$, where all passages agree
(+\FlagDiffMistralRhoOne{} to +\FlagDiffLlamaRhoOne). H3 is \emph{supported},
as an association.

\subsection{Poison types, models and further measures (RQ4)}\label{sec:rq4}
All \FFourSig{} interaction blocks of F4 are significant ($\chi^2$ from
\FFourChiRhoType{} to \FFourChiPromptModel), but the differences behind them
are small: pooled over $\rho>0$ the poison types differ by at most
\TypeSpreadPooledMax{} points within a model (\TypeSpreadMax{} at a single
$\rho$), in no consistent order, while Llama is poisoned less often
(\TypeAsrPooledLlama\,\%) than Mistral (\TypeAsrPooledMistral\,\%) and Qwen
(\TypeAsrPooledQwen\,\%). H4 is \emph{partly supported}: the interactions
exist, but authoritative framing does not make a poison more effective here.

The remaining results are exploratory. At $\rho=0.25$ Qwen is poisoned in
\PosAsrQwenFirst\,\% of cases when the single poisoned passage comes first,
against \PosAsrQwenThird--\PosAsrQwenSecond\,\% in the middle positions. With
BM25, \RetrShareLlamaEffNone\,\% of the retrieved contexts contain no
answer-bearing passage; elsewhere ASR follows the effective poisoned share.
Hallucinations are rare with controlled passages
(\HallQwenRhoZero--\HallMistralRhoOne\,\% of responses; precision
\HallPrecision, misses \HallMiss) and reach \HallLlamaRetrieval\,\% for Llama
with retrieval. Self-consistency over five samples ranges from \ScRangeMin{}
to \ScRangeMax\,\%; it is highest for Qwen and with the plain prompt, and
falls from $\rho=0$ to $\rho=1$ for every model and prompt. Without the
\AuditFlagged{} audit-flagged questions all \SensFlaggedSame{} confirmatory
conclusions hold; without the \LeakQuestions{} questions whose poison leaks
the gold answer
\SensLeaksSame{} of \SensConclusions{} hold, as Llama's reliability-prompt ASR
reduction and Mistral's consistency-prompt accuracy cost lose significance.

\section{Concluding Remarks}\label{sec:conclusions}
The three open models we tested follow retrieved evidence almost mechanically.
Their answers track the share of poisoned passages, attack success overtakes
accuracy once the false passages are the majority, and full poisoning
overrides answers the models give correctly without any passage. This matches
the majority behaviour reported by \citet{jin2024tugofwar} and the overriding
of correct priors reported by \citet{wu2024clasheval}, here measured with
paired contexts that change one passage at a time. The practical consequence
is that a 7--8B model in a RAG system is no more reliable than the share of
trustworthy passages its retriever returns.

Prompt-level defences help less than they appear to. The meta-prompts lower
attack success by a few points, mostly by making the model abstain rather than
recover the true answer, and the largest reduction comes with the largest
accuracy cost on clean evidence (Mistral with the reliability prompt): the
user gains caution, not discernment. Conflict statements are informative,
since flagged answers are poisoned less often, but a flag is neither a
guarantee nor shown to be a cause: flagged answers are still often poisoned,
and the judge that detects flags over-flags. Poison framing barely mattered;
the models differed more than the poison types.

What the study does not show matters as much. Accuracy is a bracket between
the rule-based lower bound and the judge-based upper bound, and Llama's
crossover depends on which bound is used. H1 placed the majority at
$\rho=0.5$, which with four passages is a tie; we kept the wording and report
where the change actually occurs. The four poison types share one false answer
and differ by one sentence, a weak manipulation of plausibility, and all
poisons are synthetic. The flag result is an association measured by an
imperfect judge ($\kappa=0.57$ for Qwen). Many single cells are under-powered,
so a non-significant cell is not evidence of no effect. HotpotQA contributed
comparison questions with implausible targets and some answer leaks, although
the sensitivity analyses left nearly all conclusions unchanged. The retrieval
check uses a small poisoned BM25 corpus, and all results come from three
7--8B models on one dataset.

Future work should test larger models, natural rather than synthetic conflicts
such as outdated or disputed facts, dense and hybrid retrievers over realistic
corpora, and defences that act on the passages, such as source scoring or
cross-passage verification, rather than on the prompt alone.
