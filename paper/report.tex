\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) gives a language model access to
documents it was not trained on: a retriever selects passages relevant to a
question, and the model answers with those passages in its context
\citep{lewis2020rag}. The approach improved open-domain question answering
and made generated text more specific and more factual than that of a model
without retrieval. It also moves the source of errors. A RAG system is only
as reliable as what it retrieves, and it implicitly trusts that the retrieved
text is true.

That trust is easy to break. Retrieved passages can be mistaken, out of date,
or written on purpose to mislead: \citet{zou2024poisonedrag} showed that
injecting five crafted texts per target question into a knowledge base of
millions of documents makes a RAG system return the attacker's answer in
about 90\% of cases. A recent survey organises the trustworthiness of RAG into
six dimensions, among them factuality and robustness, and observes that most
work on RAG has targeted accuracy and efficiency rather than these properties
\citep{zhou2024trustworthiness}.

Two empirical studies are closest to ours. \citet{jin2024tugofwar} studied
conflicts between a model's internal knowledge and retrieved evidence, and
found that models tend to follow the answer supported by the majority of
passages, show confirmation bias, and that stronger models can hold on to
wrong internal knowledge. \citet{wu2024clasheval} perturbed the answers in
retrieved documents across six domains and found that models adopted the
incorrect content, overriding a correct prior, more than 60\% of the time;
the less realistic the error, the less often it was adopted.

These studies establish that language models can be misled by retrieved text.
We ask a narrower, practical question: \emph{how reliably do open,
instruction-tuned language models answer when part of the retrieved evidence
is deliberately false, and can simple prompt-level instructions make them
more reliable?} A user of an open model cannot retrain it but can change what
it is told, so whether an instruction to check the passages protects, and
what it costs on clean evidence, is worth measuring. We also record whether
the model states that the passages conflict: noticing a conflict and resisting
it are not the same thing.

Our contribution is a measurement, not a new method. We use 500 multi-hop
questions from HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}, with contexts in which the
share of false passages ranges from none to all, four kinds of poison that
share one false answer and differ by a single framing sentence, and three
prompts that differ by a single sentence. Three open models of 7--8 billion
parameters answer every condition, and a standard lexical retriever over a
poisoned collection shows how often such contexts arise in practice. The
scoring rules and every confirmatory test were fixed before the main run and
validated by hand on a separate pilot set; changes to the original design are
reported in Section~\ref{sec:revisions}.

\section{Research Question and Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Goals and research questions}\label{sec:rqs}
The main question of Section~\ref{sec:intro} is split into four research
questions, all answered on the full experimental grid.
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1, robustness.] How do accuracy and attack success change as the
    share of poisoned passages grows from none to all?
  \item[RQ2, meta-prompts.] Do prompts asking the model to check the passages
    for consistency, or to judge their reliability, lower attack success, and
    at what cost in accuracy and abstentions on clean contexts?
  \item[RQ3, conflict awareness.] How often do models state that the passages
    conflict, and are such responses less often poisoned?
  \item[RQ4, types and models] (secondary). Do the answers to RQ1--RQ3 differ
    between poison types and between models?
\end{description}
The hypotheses were written before any experiment. \textbf{H1}: attack
success rises with the poison ratio, most steeply around the point where the
poisoned passages stop being outnumbered \citep{jin2024tugofwar}, and is high
with all passages poisoned even for questions answered correctly without
passages \citep{wu2024clasheval}. \textbf{H2} (our expectation): meta-prompts
help mainly when clean and poisoned passages are mixed, little when all are
poisoned, and cost some accuracy and abstentions on clean contexts.
\textbf{H3} (our expectation): conflict statements peak at intermediate
ratios, where passages disagree, and flagged responses are less often
poisoned. \textbf{H4}: more plausible or authoritative poisons are more
effective \citep{wu2024clasheval}; models differ, in no predicted direction.
The test of H1 concerns the rise only; where it is steepest is read from the
curves. RQ3 is an association: the experimenter does not control whether a
model states a conflict.

\subsection{Problem definition}\label{sec:formal}
Let $\mathcal{Q}=\{(q_i,a^{*}_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be questions with their gold
answers, and $\tilde a_i\neq a^{*}_i$ one false \emph{target answer} per
question. Each question has $k=4$ clean answer-bearing passages
$c_{i1},\dots,c_{i4}$ (the answer passage and three paraphrases), a bridge
passage $b_i$ needed for the multi-hop question and never poisoned, and, for
every poison type $\tau\in\mathcal{T}$, a poisoned twin $\tilde c^{\,\tau}_{ij}$
of each clean passage that supports $\tilde a_i$ and does not contain
$a^{*}_i$. For a poison ratio $\rho\in\mathcal{R}=\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$, the
$m=\rho k$ poisoned slots are the first $m$ entries of a random permutation
$\sigma_i$ of $\{1,\dots,4\}$ drawn once per question, and the context is
\begin{equation}
C_{\rho,\tau}(q_i)=\varphi_i\big(p_{i1},\dots,p_{i4},\,b_i\big),\qquad
p_{ij}=\begin{cases}\tilde c^{\,\tau}_{ij} & \text{if } \sigma_i^{-1}(j)\le m,\\
c_{ij} & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\varphi_i$ is a passage order fixed per question. The poisoned sets are
nested in $\rho$ and shared by all poison types, prompts and models, so
raising $\rho$ replaces one clean passage by its own twin and nothing else. A
condition $\theta=(M,\pi,\tau,\rho)$, with model $M\in\mathcal{M}$ and prompt
$\pi\in\Pi$, yields a response $r_i(\theta)=M\big(\pi,q_i,C_{\rho,\tau}(q_i)\big)$,
an outcome $o_i(\theta)\in\{\textsc{Correct},\textsc{Poisoned},\textsc{Both},
\textsc{OtherWrong},\textsc{Abstain},\textsc{NoAnswer}\}$
(Section~\ref{sec:scoring}) and a conflict flag $f_i(\theta)\in\{0,1\}$, equal
to 1 if the response says that the passages disagree or that some may be
unreliable. The accuracy is
$\mathrm{Acc}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\mathbb{1}[o_i(\theta)=\textsc{Correct}]$;
the attack success rate $\mathrm{ASR}$, the abstention rate $\mathrm{Abst}$,
the rate of missing answers and the flag rate $\mathrm{Flag}$ are defined in
the same way, always over all $N$ responses, and all comparisons are paired.

\subsection{Data and poisoned contexts}\label{sec:data}
Questions come from the development set of HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}
in the distractor setting. A question is eligible if its answer is not
\emph{yes} or \emph{no}, appears in exactly one of its two supporting
paragraphs (the answer passage), and the question is of bridge type in
HotpotQA's labels; 4{,}672 of 7{,}405 questions qualify. With a fixed seed we
drew 50 pilot questions, used only to build and validate the scoring, and 500
disjoint main questions. Three paraphrases of each answer passage were written
by Gemma-2-9B-it, a model we do not evaluate, and kept only if they retained
the gold answer and added no names or numbers; dropped details are recorded
rather than rejected, since the planned stricter rule replaced 32\% of the
questions, mostly detail-rich ones (14\% after the change). The target answer
was generated by the same helper model from the question, the gold answer and
the answer passage, and had to differ from the gold answer and be absent from
the question and the passages.

All four poison types replace every occurrence of $a^{*}$ by $\tilde a$.
\textsc{Swap} changes nothing else; \textsc{Correct}, \textsc{Authority} and
\textsc{Recency} append one sentence, drawn from five fixed templates per
type, claiming that other sources are wrong, that the fact is officially
confirmed, or that the passage is the latest update. Every poisoned passage
contains $\tilde a$ and not $a^{*}$ under the scoring normalisation. Because
language models use the start and end of a context more than the middle
\citep{liu2024lost}, the order $\varphi_i$ and the position of the bridge
passage are random per question and recorded.

A realistic-retrieval check complements the controlled contexts: for each
poison type, BM25 over all clean, poisoned and bridge passages and the
HotpotQA distractor paragraphs returns the top five passages for the question,
with ties broken in a seeded random order. In this poisoned-corpus setting
\citep{zou2024poisonedrag} the rates show which ratios realistic retrieval
produces, not how often poison occurs on the open web. HotpotQA itself has
flaws: an audit of \AuditCandidates{} questions with warning signs flagged
\AuditFlagged{}, \AuditGoldErrors{} with a wrong gold answer and
\AuditComparison{} comparison questions that passed the bridge filter. All
questions stay in the primary analysis; their influence is measured
separately.

\subsection{Models, prompts and decoding}\label{sec:models}
$\mathcal{M}$ contains three open instruction-tuned models from different
families: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, run with vLLM 0.31.0 at pinned revisions on one A100
GPU, unquantised in bfloat16 because the library no longer supported the
planned 4-bit method. Each prompt is a single user message holding the
instruction, the numbered passages without titles, the question and an output
instruction: reason briefly and end with ``Answer: $\langle$answer$\rangle$'',
or ``Answer: unknown'' if the question cannot be answered reliably. The three
prompts $\Pi$ differ by one sentence: the plain prompt only asks to answer from
the passages; the consistency prompt adds ``First check whether the passages
agree with each other, then answer.''; the reliability prompt adds ``First
judge how reliable each passage is, then answer.'' Models are never told that
passages may be false. Per model there are 48 poisoned, 3 clean ($\rho=0$,
shared by all poison types), one closed-book and 12 retrieval conditions, 192
over the three models. Primary results use greedy decoding; five samples at
temperature 0.7 per question and condition measure self-consistency. Outputs
are capped at 512 tokens.

\subsection{Scoring and statistical analysis}\label{sec:scoring}
The final answer is the text after the last ``Answer:'' in the response,
compared with the references by whole-word containment after normalisation.
Ordered rules give the outcome: no answer line, \textsc{NoAnswer};
``unknown'', \textsc{Abstain}; both $a^{*}$ and $\tilde a$, \textsc{Both};
then \textsc{Correct}, then \textsc{Poisoned}; a fixed abstention phrase,
\textsc{Abstain}; otherwise \textsc{OtherWrong}. A blind audit of pilot
answers found the \textsc{Correct}, \textsc{Poisoned} and \textsc{Abstain}
rules exact, but half of the \textsc{OtherWrong} answers were the gold or
target answer in another form \citep{kamalloo2023evaluating}: rule-based
accuracy is a lower bound. A judge re-labelling \textsc{OtherWrong} answers
agreed with hand labels ($\kappa=0.836$) but failed a check fixed in advance
(92.8\% of exact targets recognised, 95\% required), so its rates are only an
exploratory upper bound.

The conflict flag is given by Gemma-2-9B-it reading the response alone.
Against 300 blind hand labels it reached $\kappa=0.735$ (95\% CI
0.649--0.814), but it over-flags (35.0\% against 25.7\%) and agrees least for
Qwen ($\kappa=0.57$), so flags are read against each model's $\rho=0$
baseline. An \textsc{OtherWrong} answer is a hallucination if it contains a
named entity (spaCy) or a number found in no passage; this replaced a first
rule that measured wording rather than invention, and on fresh hand labels has
precision 9/13 with 0 of 18 inventions missed. Self-consistency is the share
of five samples that agree with the most frequent answer, averaged over
questions.

Rates carry Wilson 95\% intervals; differences a question-clustered bootstrap
(10{,}000 resamples). Four confirmatory families, two-sided at $\alpha=0.05$
with Holm's correction within each, were fixed with the scoring code before
the main run; logistic models use generalised estimating equations clustered
by question. \textbf{F1} (H1): per model, the effect of $\rho$ on
\textsc{Poisoned}, adjusting for the prompt. \textbf{F2} (H2): exact McNemar
tests of each meta-prompt against the plain prompt, per model, on
\textsc{Poisoned} over all poisoned conditions and on \textsc{Correct} and
\textsc{Abstain} at $\rho=0$ (18 tests). \textbf{F3} (H3): per model, the odds
ratio of \textsc{Poisoned} for flagged against unflagged responses, adjusting
for $\rho$, prompt and type. \textbf{F4} (H4): pooled over models, the
interactions of $\rho$ and prompt with type and with model. Everything else is
exploratory. The plan allowed the pilot only to raise $N$; we kept $N=500$
although a pilot power check gave a median power of 0.92 for a five-point
difference with a quarter of comparisons below 0.80, and report each cell's
minimum detectable difference instead, never reading a non-significant cell as
the absence of an effect.

\section{Experimental Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}
All 192 conditions ran on one A100 GPU in 39.1 compute units, giving 576{,}000
completions (96{,}000 greedy, 480{,}000 sampled) and 188{,}047 judge labels,
with no resumed run. The raw outputs had the same SHA-256 fingerprint on the
cloud machine and locally, and were scored by the evaluator frozen at tag
\texttt{evaluator-v1}. Rates pool prompts and poison types unless stated;
estimates with intervals are in Tables T1--T3 of the repository.

\subsection{Robustness to poison (RQ1)}\label{sec:rq1}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig1_acc_asr_vs_rho.pdf}
\caption{Accuracy (open markers) and attack success rate (filled) against the
share of poisoned passages $\rho$, per model, pooled over prompts and poison
types, with 95\% Wilson intervals. Dotted: closed-book accuracy. Vertical
lines: first tested $\rho$ with ASR above accuracy, under the rules (solid)
and the judge-based upper bound (dashed, Llama).}
\label{fig:rq1}
\end{figure}
Every model follows the passages (Fig.~\ref{fig:rq1}): from $\rho=0$ to
$\rho=1$, ASR rises from \AsrRangeRhoZeroMin--\AsrRangeRhoZeroMax\,\% to
\AsrRangeRhoOneMin--\AsrRangeRhoOneMax\,\% and accuracy falls from
\AccRangeRhoZeroMin--\AccRangeRhoZeroMax\,\% to
\AccRangeRhoOneMin--\AccRangeRhoOneMax\,\%. F1 is significant for every model
after Holm's correction, with odds ratios of \FOneOrQwen--\FOneOrMistral{}
between $\rho=0$ and $\rho=1$, large because almost no answer is poisoned at
$\rho=0$. The steepest rise (exploratory) lies between
$\rho=\StepFromMistral$ and $\StepToMistral$ for Mistral and Qwen
(+\StepMaxMistral{} and +\StepMaxQwen{} points), when the poison becomes the
majority, and between $\StepFromLlama$ and $\StepToLlama$ for Llama
(+\StepMaxLlama). H1 placed it where the poison stops being outnumbered,
$\rho=0.5$, which with four passages is a 2--2 tie: the rise holds, its
location does not, and H1 is \emph{partly supported}. Its last part holds: at
$\rho=1$ accuracy is below closed-book accuracy
(\ClosedAccMin--\ClosedAccMax\,\%), and questions answered correctly without
passages are still poisoned in \PriorGoldAsrRhoOneMin--\PriorGoldAsrRhoOneMax\,\%
of cases. Under the rules ASR first exceeds accuracy at $\rho=\CrossRulesLlama$
for every model; the judge-based upper bound adds
\BrAccGapMin--\BrAccGapMax{} points to accuracy (at most \BrAsrGapMax{} to
ASR) and moves Llama's crossover to $\rho=\CrossUpperLlama$. Missing answer
lines matter only for Llama (\NoAnswerLlama\,\%, \NoAnswerTruncLlama{} points
of them from the 512-token cap).

\subsection{Effect of meta-prompts (RQ2)}\label{sec:rq2}
Over all poisoned conditions the meta-prompts lower ASR in \FTwoAsrSig{} of
\FTwoAsrTests{} confirmatory comparisons (F2), changing it by \FTwoAsrLlamaRel{} to
\FTwoAsrMistralRel{} points; for Qwen the reliability prompt shows no
detectable effect (+\FTwoAsrQwenRel, 95\% CI \FTwoAsrQwenRelLo{} to
\FTwoAsrQwenRelHi), which rules out large effects but not small ones. On clean
contexts abstention rises in \FTwoAbstUp{} of \FTwoAbstTests{} comparisons
(+\FTwoAbstMistralCons{} to +\FTwoAbstMistralRel{} points), and accuracy falls
significantly only for Mistral (\FTwoAccMistralCons{} points with the
consistency prompt, \FTwoAccMistralRel{} with the reliability prompt). The
per-$\rho$ differences (exploratory; figure in the repository) are not
concentrated at intermediate $\rho$ for every model: for Mistral with the
reliability prompt the reduction grows with $\rho$ and is largest at $\rho=1$,
where no clean passage is left to compare against, and Llama with the
reliability prompt changes only at $\rho=1$. Of the answers poisoned under the
plain prompt, \TransStayMin--\TransStayMax\,\% stay poisoned under a
meta-prompt; for Mistral with the reliability prompt the others are mostly
abstentions (\TransMistralRelAbstain\,\%, against \TransMistralRelCorrect\,\%
correct). The prompts reduce ASR mostly by refusing, not by recovering the true
answer, and H2 is \emph{partly supported}. Single cells are noisy: the median
minimum detectable difference is \MddMedian{} points, and \MddUnderpowered{}
of \MddCells{} cells have under 80\% power for five points.

\subsection{Conflict awareness (RQ3)}\label{sec:rq3}
With $\rho$, prompt and type fixed, flagged responses are less often poisoned
for all three models (F3, significant after Holm's correction; odds ratios
\FThreeOrMistral--\FThreeOrLlama), yet flagged answers are still poisoned in
\FThreeAsrFlaggedMistral--\FThreeAsrFlaggedQwen\,\% of cases: the test shows
an association, not that noticing a conflict prevents poisoning. Flags are
read against each model's clean baseline
(\FlagBaseMistral--\FlagBaseLlama\,\%, partly inflated by the judge's over-flagging).
Above it they peak at $\rho=0.5$, where clean and poisoned passages are tied
(+\FlagDiffMistralRhoHalf{} to +\FlagDiffLlamaRhoHalf{} points), and fall at
$\rho=1$, where all passages agree (+\FlagDiffMistralRhoOne{} to
+\FlagDiffLlamaRhoOne). H3 is \emph{supported}, as an association.

\subsection{Poison types, models and further measures (RQ4)}\label{sec:rq4}
All \FFourSig{} interaction blocks of F4 are significant ($\chi^2$ from
\FFourChiRhoType{} to \FFourChiPromptModel), but the differences behind them
are small: pooled over $\rho>0$ the poison types differ by at most
\TypeSpreadPooledMax{} points within a model (\TypeSpreadMax{} at a single
$\rho$), in no consistent order, while Llama is poisoned less often
(\TypeAsrPooledLlama\,\%) than Mistral (\TypeAsrPooledMistral\,\%) and Qwen
(\TypeAsrPooledQwen\,\%). H4 is \emph{partly supported}: the interactions
exist, but authoritative framing does not make a poison more effective here.

Exploratory results follow. At $\rho=0.25$ Qwen is poisoned in
\PosAsrQwenFirst\,\% of cases when the single poisoned passage comes first,
against \PosAsrQwenThird--\PosAsrQwenSecond\,\% in the middle positions. With
BM25, \RetrShareLlamaEffNone\,\% of the retrieved contexts contain no
answer-bearing passage; elsewhere ASR follows the effective poisoned share.
Hallucinations are rare with controlled passages
(\HallQwenRhoZero--\HallMistralRhoOne\,\% of responses; precision
\HallPrecision, misses \HallMiss) and reach \HallLlamaRetrieval\,\% for Llama
with retrieval. Self-consistency over five samples ranges from \ScRangeMin{}
to \ScRangeMax\,\% (outcome agreement \ScOutcomeRangeMin--\ScOutcomeRangeMax\,\%);
it is highest for Qwen and with the plain prompt, and falls from $\rho=0$ to
$\rho=1$ for every model and prompt. Without the \AuditFlagged{}
audit-flagged questions all \SensFlaggedSame{} confirmatory conclusions hold;
without the \LeakQuestions{} questions whose poison leaks the gold answer (ASR
\LeakDiffMistral--\LeakDiffLlama{} points lower on them) \SensLeaksSame{} of
\SensConclusions{} hold, as Llama's reliability-prompt ASR reduction and
Mistral's consistency-prompt accuracy cost lose significance.
\section{Concluding Remarks}\label{sec:conclusions}
The three open models we tested follow retrieved evidence almost mechanically.
Their answers track the share of poisoned passages, attack success overtakes
accuracy once the false passages are the majority, and full poisoning
overrides answers the models give correctly without any passage. This matches
the majority behaviour reported by \citet{jin2024tugofwar} and the overriding
of correct priors reported by \citet{wu2024clasheval}, here measured with
paired contexts that change one passage at a time. The practical consequence
is that a 7--8B model in a RAG system is no more reliable than the share of
trustworthy passages its retriever returns.

Prompt-level defences help less than they appear to. The meta-prompts lower
attack success by a few points, mostly by making the model abstain rather than
recover the true answer, and the largest reduction comes with the largest
accuracy cost on clean evidence (Mistral with the reliability prompt): the
user gains caution, not discernment. Conflict statements are informative,
since flagged answers are poisoned less often, but a flag is neither a
guarantee nor shown to be a cause: flagged answers are still often poisoned,
and the judge that detects flags over-flags. Poison framing barely mattered;
the models differed more than the poison types.

What the study does not show matters as much. Accuracy is a bracket between
the rule-based lower bound and the judge-based upper bound, and Llama's
crossover depends on which bound is used. H1 placed the majority at
$\rho=0.5$, which with four passages is a tie; we kept the wording and report
where the change actually occurs. The four poison types share one false answer
and differ by one sentence, a weak manipulation of plausibility, and all
poisons are synthetic. The flag result is an association measured by an
imperfect judge ($\kappa=0.57$ for Qwen). Many single cells are under-powered,
so a non-significant cell is not evidence of no effect. HotpotQA contributed
comparison questions with implausible targets and some answer leaks, although
the sensitivity analyses left nearly all conclusions unchanged. The retrieval
check uses a small poisoned BM25 corpus, and all results come from three
7--8B models on one dataset.

Future work should test larger models, natural rather than synthetic conflicts
such as outdated or disputed facts, dense and hybrid retrievers over realistic
corpora, and defences that act on the passages, such as source scoring or
cross-passage verification, rather than on the prompt alone.
