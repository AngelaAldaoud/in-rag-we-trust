\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) gives a language model access to
documents it was not trained on: a retriever selects passages relevant to a
question, and the model answers with those passages in its context
\citep{lewis2020rag}. The approach improved open-domain question answering
and made generated text more specific and more factual than that of a model
without retrieval. It also moves the source of errors. A RAG system is only
as reliable as what it retrieves, and it implicitly trusts that the retrieved
text is true.

That trust is easy to break. Retrieved passages can be mistaken, out of date,
or written on purpose to mislead: \citet{zou2024poisonedrag} showed that
injecting five crafted texts per target question into a knowledge base of
millions of documents makes a RAG system return the attacker's answer in
about 90\% of cases. A recent survey organises the trustworthiness of RAG into
six dimensions, among them factuality and robustness, and observes that most
work on RAG has targeted accuracy and efficiency rather than these properties
\citep{zhou2024trustworthiness}.

Two empirical studies are closest to ours. \citet{jin2024tugofwar} studied
conflicts between a model's internal knowledge and retrieved evidence, and
found that models tend to follow the answer supported by the majority of
passages, show confirmation bias, and that stronger models can hold on to
wrong internal knowledge. \citet{wu2024clasheval} perturbed the answers in
retrieved documents across six domains and found that models adopted the
incorrect content, overriding a correct prior, more than 60\% of the time;
the less realistic the error, the less often it was adopted. Evidence-based
systems such as the fact-checking pipeline of \citet{singal2024evidence},
which retrieves evidence and lets a language model judge a claim against it,
depend on exactly this behaviour being sound.

These studies establish that language models can be misled by retrieved text.
We ask a narrower, practical question: \emph{how reliably do open,
instruction-tuned language models answer when part of the retrieved evidence
is deliberately false, and can simple prompt-level instructions make them
more reliable?} A user of an open model cannot retrain it but can change what
it is told, so whether an instruction to check the passages for consistency,
or to judge their reliability, gives any protection, and what it costs when
the evidence is clean, is worth measuring directly. Alongside the final answer
we record whether the model states that the passages conflict, because
noticing a conflict and resisting it are not the same thing.

Our contribution is a measurement, not a new method. We use 500 multi-hop
questions from HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}, with contexts in which the
share of false passages ranges from none to all, four kinds of poison that
share one false answer and differ by a single framing sentence, and three
prompts that differ by a single sentence. Three open models of 7--8 billion
parameters answer every condition. The scoring rules and every confirmatory
test were fixed before the main run, and the scoring was validated by hand on
a separate pilot set; the changes made to the original design along the way
are reported in Section~\ref{sec:revisions}. A standard lexical retriever over
a poisoned collection shows how often such contexts arise in practice.

\section{Research Question and Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Goals and research questions}\label{sec:rqs}
The main question of Section~\ref{sec:intro} is split into four research
questions, all answered on the full experimental grid.
\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1, robustness.] How do accuracy and attack success change as the
    share of poisoned passages grows from none to all?
  \item[RQ2, meta-prompts.] Do prompts that ask the model to check the
    passages for consistency, or to judge their reliability, lower attack
    success compared with a plain prompt, and what do they cost in accuracy
    and abstentions on clean contexts?
  \item[RQ3, conflict awareness.] How often do models state that the passages
    conflict, and are responses that do so less often poisoned?
  \item[RQ4, poison types and models] (secondary). Do the answers to RQ1--RQ3
    differ between poison types and between models?
\end{description}
The hypotheses were written before any experiment. \textbf{H1}: attack
success rises with the poison ratio, most steeply around the point where the
poisoned passages stop being outnumbered, following the majority rule of
\citet{jin2024tugofwar}; with all passages poisoned it is high even for
questions the model answers correctly without passages, as models often
override a correct prior \citep{wu2024clasheval}. \textbf{H2} (our
expectation): meta-prompts help mainly when clean and poisoned passages are
mixed, help little when every passage is poisoned, and cost some accuracy and
add abstentions on clean contexts. \textbf{H3} (our expectation): conflict
statements are most frequent at intermediate ratios, the only ones where
passages disagree with each other, and flagged responses are less often
poisoned. \textbf{H4}: more plausible or more authoritative poisons are more
effective \citep{wu2024clasheval}; models differ, but we predict no direction.
The confirmatory test of H1 concerns the rise only; where it is steepest is
read from the curves. RQ3 is an association: the experimenter does not control
whether a model states a conflict.

\subsection{Problem definition}\label{sec:formal}
Let $\mathcal{Q}=\{(q_i,a^{*}_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ be questions with their gold
answers, and $\tilde a_i\neq a^{*}_i$ one false \emph{target answer} per
question. Each question has $k=4$ clean answer-bearing passages
$c_{i1},\dots,c_{i4}$ (the original answer passage and three paraphrases), a
bridge passage $b_i$ needed for the multi-hop question and never poisoned, and,
for every poison type $\tau\in\mathcal{T}$, a poisoned twin
$\tilde c^{\,\tau}_{ij}$ of each clean passage that supports $\tilde a_i$ and
does not contain $a^{*}_i$. For a poison ratio
$\rho\in\mathcal{R}=\{0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$, the $m=\rho k$ poisoned slots are
the first $m$ entries of a random permutation $\sigma_i$ of $\{1,\dots,4\}$
drawn once per question, and the context is
\begin{equation}
C_{\rho,\tau}(q_i)=\varphi_i\big(p_{i1},\dots,p_{i4},\,b_i\big),\qquad
p_{ij}=\begin{cases}\tilde c^{\,\tau}_{ij} & \text{if } \sigma_i^{-1}(j)\le m,\\
c_{ij} & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\varphi_i$ is a passage order fixed per question. The poisoned sets are
nested in $\rho$ and identical for every poison type, prompt and model, so
raising $\rho$ replaces one clean passage by its own twin and changes nothing
else. A condition is $\theta=(M,\pi,\tau,\rho)$ with model $M\in\mathcal{M}$
and prompt $\pi\in\Pi$; it yields the response
$r_i(\theta)=M\big(\pi,q_i,C_{\rho,\tau}(q_i)\big)$, one outcome
$o_i(\theta)\in\{\textsc{Correct},\textsc{Poisoned},\textsc{Both},
\textsc{OtherWrong},\textsc{Abstain},\textsc{NoAnswer}\}$
(Section~\ref{sec:scoring}), and a conflict flag $f_i(\theta)\in\{0,1\}$, equal
to 1 if the response states that the passages disagree or that some may be
unreliable. The main rates are
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Acc}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}\big[o_i(\theta)=\textsc{Correct}\big],\qquad
\mathrm{ASR}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{1}\big[o_i(\theta)=\textsc{Poisoned}\big],
\end{equation}
the attack success rate, and analogously the abstention rate
$\mathrm{Abst}$, the rate of missing answers and the flag rate
$\mathrm{Flag}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_i f_i(\theta)$. Every rate keeps all $N$
responses in its denominator, so a response without an answer can never raise
a score. The problem is to estimate how these rates change when one factor of
$\theta$ varies and the others are fixed. Because every condition uses the same
questions, all comparisons are paired.

\subsection{Data and poisoned contexts}\label{sec:data}
Questions come from the development set of HotpotQA \citep{yang2018hotpotqa}
in the distractor setting. A question is eligible if its answer is not
\emph{yes} or \emph{no}, appears in exactly one of its two supporting
paragraphs (the answer passage), and the question is of bridge type in
HotpotQA's labels; 4{,}672 of 7{,}405 questions qualify. With a fixed seed we
drew 50 pilot questions, used only to build and validate the scoring, and 500
disjoint main questions. Three paraphrases of each answer passage were written
by Gemma-2-9B-it, a model we do not evaluate, and kept only if they retained
the gold answer and added no names or numbers; 27 of 30 accepted paraphrases
checked by hand were faithful. The target answer was written by the same
helper model from the question, the gold answer and the answer passage, and
had to differ from the gold answer and be absent from the question and the
passages. We first drew targets from gold answers of other questions with the
same entity type, but a manual check found only 5 of 30 plausible, against 19
of 30 for the generated targets on the same questions.

All four poison types replace every occurrence of $a^{*}$ by $\tilde a$.
\textsc{Swap} changes nothing else; \textsc{Correct}, \textsc{Authority} and
\textsc{Recency} append one sentence, drawn from five fixed templates per
type, claiming that other sources are wrong, that the fact is officially
confirmed (e.g.\ ``This information is confirmed by official government
records.''), or that the passage is the latest update. Every poisoned passage
is checked to contain $\tilde a$ and not to contain $a^{*}$ under the
normalisation used for scoring. Because language models use information at
the start and end of a context more than in the middle \citep{liu2024lost},
the order $\varphi_i$ is random per question, the bridge passage takes a
random position, and every position is recorded.

A realistic-retrieval check complements the controlled contexts. For each
poison type, a BM25 index over 9{,}301 passages (the clean, poisoned and bridge
passages of all questions and the HotpotQA distractor paragraphs) returns the
top five passages for the question text, with ties broken in a seeded random
order. This is a poisoned-corpus setting in the sense of
\citet{zou2024poisonedrag}: its rates show which ratios realistic retrieval
produces, not how often poison occurs on the open web. HotpotQA itself has
flaws: one comparison question is mislabelled as bridge, and some gold answers
are wrong; all questions stay in the primary analysis, and their influence is
measured separately.

\subsection{Models, prompts and decoding}\label{sec:models}
$\mathcal{M}$ contains three open instruction-tuned models from different
families: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, run unquantised in bfloat16 with vLLM 0.31.0 at
pinned revisions, all on one A100 GPU. Each prompt is a single user message
holding the instruction, the numbered passages without titles, the question
and an output instruction: reason briefly and end with
``Answer: $\langle$answer$\rangle$'', or ``Answer: unknown'' if the question
cannot be answered reliably. The three prompts $\Pi$ differ by one sentence:
the plain prompt only asks to answer from the passages; the consistency prompt
adds ``First check whether the passages agree with each other, then
answer.''; the reliability prompt adds ``First judge how reliable each passage
is, then answer.'' Models are never told that passages may be false. Each
model answers 48 poisoned conditions, 3 clean ones ($\rho=0$, shared by all
poison types), one closed-book condition without passages and 12 retrieval
conditions: 192 conditions over the three models. Primary results use greedy
decoding; five samples at temperature 0.7 per question and condition measure
self-consistency. Outputs are capped at 512 tokens.

\subsection{Scoring and statistical analysis}\label{sec:scoring}
The final answer is the text after the last ``Answer:'' anywhere in the
response. Answers and references are compared by whole-word containment after
standard normalisation, with bracketed remarks removed. Ordered rules assign
the outcome: no answer line is \textsc{NoAnswer}; ``unknown'' is
\textsc{Abstain}; an answer containing both $a^{*}$ and $\tilde a$ is
\textsc{Both}; then \textsc{Correct}, then \textsc{Poisoned}; a fixed
abstention phrase (``not mentioned'', ``cannot be answered'', \dots) is
\textsc{Abstain}; anything else is \textsc{OtherWrong}. A blind hand audit of
pilot answers found the \textsc{Correct}, \textsc{Poisoned} and
\textsc{Abstain} rules exact (35/35), but only 10 of 20 \textsc{OtherWrong}
answers truly wrong: most were the gold or target answer in another form, the
known weakness of lexical matching \citep{kamalloo2023evaluating}. Rule-based
accuracy is therefore a lower bound. A judge re-examining \textsc{OtherWrong}
answers agreed with hand labels ($\kappa=0.836$) but failed a check fixed in
advance, recognising the target in only 92.8\% of exact cases against a
required 95\%; its re-labelled rates are reported only as an exploratory upper
bound.

The conflict flag is assigned by Gemma-2-9B-it, which reads the response alone
and answers whether it says that the passages disagree or that some may be
unreliable. Against 300 blind hand labels it reached $\kappa=0.735$ (95\% CI
0.649--0.814). It over-flags (35.0\% against 25.7\% by hand) and agrees least
for Qwen ($\kappa=0.57$), so flag rates are read against their $\rho=0$
baseline. An \textsc{OtherWrong} answer counts as a hallucination if it
contains a named entity (found with spaCy) or a number that appears in no
passage; on fresh hand labels this rule had precision 9/13 and missed 0 of 18
inventions. Self-consistency is the share of the five samples that agree with
the most frequent answer, averaged over questions.

Single rates carry Wilson 95\% intervals; differences use a paired bootstrap
over questions (10{,}000 resamples). Four families of confirmatory tests,
two-sided at $\alpha=0.05$ with Holm's correction within each family, were
fixed with the scoring code before the main run. Logistic regressions use
generalised estimating equations clustered by question. \textbf{F1} (H1), per
model: the effect of $\rho$ on \textsc{Poisoned}, adjusting for the prompt.
\textbf{F2} (H2): exact McNemar tests of each meta-prompt against the plain
prompt, per model, on \textsc{Poisoned} over all poisoned conditions and on
\textsc{Correct} and \textsc{Abstain} at $\rho=0$ (18 tests). \textbf{F3}
(H3), per model: the odds ratio of \textsc{Poisoned} for flagged against
unflagged responses, adjusting for $\rho$, prompt and type. \textbf{F4} (H4):
on all models pooled, the interactions of $\rho$ and prompt with poison type
and with model. All other analyses are exploratory. At $N=500$ the median
comparison of single conditions has power 0.92 for a five-point difference,
but about a quarter fall below 0.80; we therefore report each cell's minimum
detectable difference and never read a non-significant single-cell difference
as the absence of an effect.
